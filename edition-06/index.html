Why this edition exists

Edition 05 showed how exposed creators are to platforms, sponsors and advertisers. Edition 06 follows the creators who answered that by owning something: a subscription, a cosmetics line, a hot sauce, a football club, a restaurant chain, a chocolate bar. It asks what they built, who they built it with, and what the record shows it was worth.

01 / The finding

The creators who lasted owned something besides the audience.

Twelve of these fifteen cases are about ownership: a creator who built, bought back or raised money for a business of their own. Two moved an audience into the physical world, with restaurants and hot sauce, and one built a football club.

Eleven are archival, a launch, an acquisition or a valuation, and three show revenue moving: Feastables at $250 million, Ipsy at $500 million a year and First We Feast earning 85% of its money outside advertising. One rests on a billion views and downloads.

Valuations here are large, $1.2 billion, $1.8 billion and $1 billion, but they were set by investors and buyers, not by published accounts. The strongest cases had a partner who could make, ship or run the thing the audience wanted.

A note on scope

What Edition 06 leans on

All 15 cases run from 2011 to 2024. Ten began in the United States, four in Britain and one in Dubai. Three founders, Kylie Jenner, Hailey Bieber and to a degree Emily Weiss, came to fame outside YouTube, but each built a brand the way creators do, through their own channels. Creator brands already covered in the sister archives, including Prime, MrBeast Burger and Gymshark, are not repeated.

01 MrBeast / 2022 / OwnershipFeastablesThe biggest creator on YouTube launched a chocolate bar. Within three years it earned more than his videos.YouTube · Bigger than the channel $250 millionFeastables sales in 2024, as reportedSales trend

North Carolina, United States / Confectionery

MrBeast, 2022 to 2024

The situation

MrBeast’s videos cost millions to make and earned mostly from advertising, which barely covered production. His first food venture, MrBeast Burger, had run into quality problems and legal disputes.

The move

Jimmy Donaldson launched Feastables in January 2022 with simple chocolate bars, promoted through his videos and challenges, then built distribution through Walmart, Target and other retailers.

  • MrBeast The largest audience on YouTube and a way of making a product part of the entertainment.
  • Retail partners Shelf space across the United States and beyond.

The turning point

An investor presentation reported in 2025 showed Feastables made about $250 million in sales in 2024 and turned a profit, while the media business lost money. The product had become the business.

My take

Feastables is the end point of the creator economy so far: the videos are the marketing, and the chocolate pays for them. Burger taught the lesson Feastables applied, which is to own the product properly.

02 Michelle Phan / 2011 / OwnershipIpsyShe taught millions to do their make-up on YouTube. Then she sent them a bag of products every month.YouTube · Company built $500 millionIpsy’s reported annual revenue, 2017Sales trend

California, United States / Beauty

Michelle Phan, 2011

The situation

Beauty tutorials were among YouTube’s most watched videos, and beauty brands paid creators to feature products. Few creators owned the business that profited from their recommendations.

The move

Michelle Phan, one of YouTube’s first beauty stars, co-founded Ipsy in 2011, a monthly subscription that sent subscribers a bag of sample-sized products chosen for them.

  • Michelle Phan Millions of viewers who trusted her taste and a deep network of beauty creators.
  • Brands Products supplied for the bags, in return for reaching an engaged audience.

The turning point

Ipsy raised $100 million in 2015, valuing it at about $500 million, and by 2017 was reported to have about $500 million in annual revenue, with creators at the centre of its marketing.

My take

Phan was early to the idea that a creator’s audience is a customer base. Ipsy made recommendation a business model, with other creators as its sales force.

03 Deliciously Ella / 2012 / OwnershipFrom Blog to Supermarket ShelfA student started a recipe blog while learning to cook differently. It became one of Britain’s best-known plant-based brands.Blog · Brand built 2012When the blog began · legacy factArchival

London, United Kingdom / Food

Ella Mills, 2012

The situation

Plant-based food was a niche in British supermarkets. Cookery books came from television chefs, and food brands from food companies.

The move

Ella Mills started Deliciously Ella in 2012 as a blog of plant-based recipes, written while she changed her diet after illness. It grew into cookbooks, an app, a deli and then packaged food.

  • Ella Mills Recipes, honesty about why she started and a loyal community of readers.
  • Retail partners Supermarket shelf space for snacks and meals under her name.

The turning point

Her first cookbook, in 2015, was a bestseller, and Deliciously Ella products went on sale in major supermarkets. A blog had become a brand sold far beyond its readers.

My take

Deliciously Ella is the British template for creator to consumer brand. It worked because the products made sense on a supermarket shelf, not only on a blog.

04 Huda Kattan / 2013 / OwnershipHuda BeautyA make-up artist in Dubai started with false lashes and a blog. Investors later valued her company at more than a billion dollars.Instagram · Valued at a billion $1.2 billionReported valuation, December 2017 · legacy factArchival

Dubai, United Arab Emirates / Beauty

Huda Kattan, 2013

The situation

Global beauty brands were built in Paris and New York. A make-up artist with a blog in Dubai had no obvious route to an international brand.

The move

Huda Kattan wrote a beauty blog and posted tutorials on Instagram, then launched false eyelashes under the Huda Beauty name in 2013, funded with a loan from her sister.

  • Huda Kattan Expertise, an enormous Instagram following and instinct for what her audience wanted.
  • Retailers Distribution through Sephora and others that took the brand worldwide.

The turning point

In December 2017 TSG Consumer Partners bought a minority stake, in a deal reported to value Huda Beauty at about $1.2 billion. In 2025 Kattan bought back full ownership.

My take

Huda Beauty shows a creator building a global brand from Dubai, then taking it back. Ownership was the point from the start.

05 Into the Gloss / 2014 / OwnershipGlossierA beauty blog asked readers what they wanted. Its founder built the products and a company worth $1.8 billion.Blog · Valued at $1.8 billion $1.8 billionValuation at the July 2021 funding round · legacy factArchival

New York, United States / Beauty

Emily Weiss, 2010 to 2021

The situation

Beauty brands decided what to sell and advertised it. Customers were rarely asked before a product was made.

The move

Emily Weiss founded Into the Gloss in 2010, interviewing women about their routines. In 2014 she launched Glossier, products shaped by readers’ comments and sold directly online.

  • Emily Weiss and Into the Gloss An engaged readership and years of conversation about what people wanted.
  • Investors Funding to build products, shops and a brand around the community.

The turning point

Glossier raised $80 million in July 2021 at a $1.8 billion valuation. The company later cut staff and reset, and Weiss stepped back as chief executive in 2022.

My take

Glossier is the community-first brand everyone copied. Its later struggles are a lesson too: what a community loves at 100,000 customers can be hard to keep at ten million.

Los Angeles, United States / Beauty

Kylie Jenner, 2015

The situation

Celebrity beauty lines usually licensed a famous name to an established company. Few were sold mainly through the celebrity’s own social media.

The move

Kylie Jenner launched lip kits in November 2015, announcing them on Instagram and Snapchat and selling them online, with a manufacturer handling production.

  • Kylie Jenner Huge social audiences and a launch that felt like news.
  • Her partners Manufacturing, fulfilment and, later, retail distribution.

The turning point

In November 2019 Coty bought 51% of her beauty business for $600 million, valuing it at about $1.2 billion. Questions were later raised about how large the business had been reported to be.

My take

Kylie Cosmetics is the case that made every celebrity think like a creator. The audience was the distribution, and the deal priced it.

07 Hot Ones / 2015 / CrossoverThe Show with Questions and Hot WingsCelebrities answered questions while eating ever-hotter wings. The hot sauces became a business of their own.YouTube · Sauce line 85%Share of First We Feast revenue from outside advertising, as reportedSales trend

New York, United States / Food and entertainment

First We Feast and Sean Evans, 2015

The situation

Celebrity interviews followed a press-junket script. Online food channels mostly lived on advertising.

The move

First We Feast launched Hot Ones in 2015, with Sean Evans interviewing guests while they ate wings with ten increasingly hot sauces. The show partnered with Heatonist to sell the sauces, then made its own, including The Last Dab.

  • Sean Evans and First We Feast Deep research, a format that made guests drop their guard and a recognisable set of sauces.
  • Heatonist A hot sauce shop that made and sold the line.

The turning point

The sauces sold out repeatedly, and Digiday reported that First We Feast earned about 85% of its revenue outside advertising, much of it from products tied to the show.

My take

Hot Ones is the best example of a show whose props became the business. Every episode is an advert that nobody skips.

Essex, United Kingdom / Football

Spencer Owen, 2016

The situation

Football clubs have histories measured in decades. A team created for YouTube videos seemed like a novelty that would never play competitive football.

The move

Spencer Owen founded Hashtag United in 2016 to play exhibition matches filmed for YouTube. In 2018 it joined the English football pyramid, with a fanbase built online.

  • Spencer Owen A YouTube audience and the idea that fans could follow a club from its first match.
  • The English football pyramid A route from the lowest league upwards, open to any club that meets the rules.

The turning point

The club won promotions, reaching the Isthmian League by 2022, and launched a women’s team. A YouTube channel had become a real club with real fixtures.

My take

Hashtag United is my favourite British case in this edition. It took the oldest kind of community, a football club, and built one from a YouTube channel.

London, United Kingdom / Podcasting and media

Steven Bartlett, 2017 to 2024

The situation

British podcasts were dominated by broadcasters and comedians. Long interviews with business leaders were seen as niche.

The move

Steven Bartlett, co-founder of the agency Social Chain, launched The Diary of a CEO in 2017, then invested in filming it as a long-form video show for YouTube and building his own company, Flight Story, around it.

Apple named it the UK’s number one podcast of 2024, it became the most subscribed podcast on YouTube’s charts, and it passed a billion views and downloads.

My take

Bartlett treats the podcast as the top of a business funnel he owns. That is the creator-as-company model, built in Britain.

Celebrity beauty brands were crowded, and many struggled after launch. A brand with a handful of products seemed an unlikely acquisition target.

The move

Hailey Bieber launched Rhode in June 2022 with a few products, sold online and promoted through her own posts and the get-ready videos of creators who liked them.

  • Hailey Bieber Her own audience, personal style and close involvement in the products.
  • e.l.f. Beauty A buyer with retail scale and experience growing social-first brands.

The turning point

In May 2025 e.l.f. Beauty agreed to buy Rhode for up to $1 billion, including future payments, saying the brand had about $212 million in net sales in the previous year.

My take

Rhode proves the creator model now runs the beauty industry. The founder posts, creators copy, and the brand grows without a traditional campaign.

Ian Hecox and Anthony Padilla, 2005 to 2023

The situation

Smosh, one of YouTube’s first stars, had been sold to a media company, which collapsed in 2018. The brand was bought by Mythical Entertainment and kept going, but the founders no longer owned it.

The move

In June 2023 Ian Hecox and Anthony Padilla, who had left in 2017, bought Smosh back from Mythical and returned to making it together.

  • Ian Hecox and Anthony Padilla The original creative partnership and a reason for old fans to come back.
  • Smosh’s cast and audience A brand that had survived several owners and still had a large audience.

The turning point

The founders reunited on screen and Smosh signed with a major talent agency, positioning the brand for a new phase under creator ownership.

My take

Smosh appears in Edition 02 as the top name in a 2014 survey. Its story closes the loop on ownership: built by creators, sold, broken and bought back by the creators who started it.

Dude Perfect, 2009 to 2024

The situation

Dude Perfect started in 2009 with trick-shot videos and grew into one of YouTube’s biggest sports entertainment channels, with live tours, merchandise and a family audience. Creators rarely raised outside capital at that scale.

The deal was the first institutional investment in the company and one of the largest in a creator business. It treated a YouTube channel as an entertainment company with a long future.

Dude Perfect closes the edition because it shows where creator brands are going: from videos, to products, to companies that raise money like any other.

04 / The evidence

What a creator brand can and can’t prove

Moment, milestone, result

A first upload or a record is a moment. A milestone, like a subscriber count, shows attention. Only a result shows what changed for the creator, the platform or a brand. Each case says which of the three the record supports.

Whose number it is

Many figures come from the platforms, the creators themselves or the press. The source for every outcome is named, so you can judge it. Estimates of creator income are marked as estimates.

Attention is not income

Views, subscribers and concurrent viewers count attention. Paid subscriptions, sales and chart positions get closer to money. The rating says which kind of number a case rests on.

The evidence rating

Every case gets one of five ratings, strongest first: modelled effect, sales trend, survey, reach only and archival. A date or a deal term is marked as a legacy fact so it isn’t read as a result.

The creator comes first

Each case puts the creator at the centre: what they made, what changed because of it, and who else was involved. Brand partnerships appear when the creator drove them.

How cases earn a place

Each case changed what was possible for creators, platforms or brands, and left a public record. Creator stories already told in Brand Turning Points or Partnership Turning Points are not repeated here; The Index links to them.
