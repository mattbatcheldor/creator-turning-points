Why this edition exists

The first four editions looked at what worked. Edition 05 looks at what broke: advertisers who left, deals that ended, products that weren’t ready, disclosures that weren’t made and platforms that switched off. Each case asks what the mistake was and what changed because of it.

01 / The finding

Most of the creator economy’s rules were written after a failure.

Thirteen of these fifteen cases are archival: a ruling, a closure, a deal cancelled or a public row. Two rest on attention, a channel of 20 million left silent and three million users who uninstalled a sponsor’s extension. Failures leave records, not measurements.

Six are about rules. The first FTC case against individual influencers, the UK’s disclosure commitments, a $170 million children’s privacy settlement and brand safety rules all followed failures. Seven are about trust: the credibility a creator lends, or loses, when the thing they promote doesn’t hold up.

The pattern is that creators carry the risk. They lost income when advertisers left, audiences when platforms closed and reputations when sponsors misbehaved. The ones who recovered fastest had more than one way to reach their audience.

London, United Kingdom / Online video and advertising

YouTube creators, 2017

The situation

YouTube placed adverts automatically across millions of videos. Brands assumed their adverts appeared beside safe content, and creators assumed advertising would keep flowing.

The move

In early 2017 The Times reported that adverts for major brands and the UK government were appearing next to extremist content. Hundreds of advertisers paused spending on YouTube.

  • The advertisers Budgets they could withdraw overnight, and a demand for brand safety.
  • YouTube Tighter rules on which videos could carry adverts, applied largely by automated systems.

The turning point

More than 250 advertisers paused spending. YouTube responded by stopping adverts on far more videos, and many ordinary creators saw their income drop sharply. The creators called it the Adpocalypse.

My take

The Adpocalypse is why so many creators sell merchandise, memberships and products today. It taught a generation that advertising income can vanish because of something they never made.

What to take into a creator programme

How I’d measure it now

For brand safety, measure where adverts actually run, not just where they are targeted. For creators, track the share of income from sources other than platform advertising.

San Francisco, United States / Short video

Twitter, 2016

The situation

Vine’s biggest creators had asked for ways to earn money on the app. Many were making a living from brand deals instead, and had begun moving to YouTube and Instagram.

The move

On 27 October 2016 Twitter announced it would discontinue the Vine app, as part of cost cuts and job losses. Vine had been bought by Twitter in 2012 and had never paid its creators directly.

  • Vine’s creators Six-second comedy that had built huge young audiences.
  • Twitter A platform it could not afford to run and had not given creators a reason to stay on.

The turning point

The app closed to new uploads in January 2017. Its best creators had already moved, and TikTok later took up the short, looping format Vine had pioneered.

My take

Vine didn’t fail because the format was wrong. TikTok proved the format was right. It failed because its creators were building careers somewhere else.

How I’d measure it now

03 2017YouTube / Gaming and comedy videos PewDiePie / 2017 / TrustDropped by DisneyThe biggest creator on YouTube made antisemitic jokes and called it satire. Brighton, United Kingdom / Online video

PewDiePie, 2017

The situation

PewDiePie was signed to Disney’s Maker Studios and had a series on YouTube’s paid service. Edgy humour had helped build his audience, and he saw his jokes as aimed at the media, not at anyone else.

The move

In February 2017 The Wall Street Journal reported that nine of his videos since August 2016 contained antisemitic jokes or Nazi imagery, including paying people to hold up an offensive sign.

  • Felix Kjellberg The largest audience of any individual on YouTube, and a defence that the jokes were taken out of context.
  • Disney and YouTube Commercial deals and brand reputations they were not prepared to risk.

The turning point

Maker Studios ended its partnership, YouTube cancelled the second season of his series Scare PewDiePie and removed him from its premium advertising programme. He apologised for the jokes while criticising the reporting.

My take

This is the case that showed brands the limits of working with personalities they don’t control. It also showed that losing brand deals doesn’t mean losing an audience.

United States / Gaming and gambling

Federal Trade Commission, 2017

The situation

Disclosure rules for endorsements existed, but regulators had pursued brands rather than the creators who posted. Many creators did not disclose paid or owned relationships.

The move

Trevor Martin and Thomas Cassell, known as TmarTn and Syndicate, promoted CSGO Lotto, a site for gambling with video game items, to their young audiences without saying they owned it.

  • The creators Large gaming audiences, many of them teenagers.
  • The Federal Trade Commission Endorsement rules, enforced against individuals for the first time.

The turning point

In September 2017 the FTC settled its first complaint against individual social media influencers, requiring the pair to disclose material connections clearly in future. It also sent warning letters to other influencers.

My take

This case ended the idea that rules applied only to advertisers. Every disclosure label in a brief today has its roots here.

How I’d measure it now

Audit a sample of creator posts each month for clear disclosure, and report the compliance rate alongside campaign results.

Exumas, Bahamas / Events

Fyre Festival, 2016 to 2017

The situation

Fyre Festival promised a luxury music festival on a private island in the Bahamas. Its organisers had little infrastructure but a large marketing budget.

The move

In December 2016 the organisers paid influencers and models, including some of the world’s best-known, to post a plain orange tile and promotional video. Most posts did not say they were paid.

  • The influencers Glamour and huge audiences, with little sight of what they were promoting.
  • The organisers Money for posts, and a festival that collapsed when guests arrived in April 2017.

The turning point

Guests found tents and emergency supplies instead of villas. The founder was later jailed for fraud, and Kendall Jenner, reported to have been paid $250,000 for one post, settled with the bankruptcy trustee.

My take

Fyre is the cautionary tale of influencer marketing as pure reach. The orange tile worked brilliantly as an advert. It just advertised something that wasn’t real.

Japan and United States / Online video

Logan Paul and YouTube, 2018

The situation

Logan Paul’s daily vlogs drew millions of young viewers, and he was part of Google Preferred, the programme that sold advertisers the platform’s most popular channels.

The move

On 31 December 2017 he posted a vlog filmed in a forest in Japan that showed a person who had died. It was viewed millions of times before he took it down.

  • Logan Paul A huge young audience and a vlog that crossed a line most viewers recognised at once.
  • YouTube Rules on what can be shown, and commercial programmes it could withdraw.

The turning point

On 10 January 2018 YouTube removed his channels from Google Preferred and put his original projects on hold. It later tightened the rules for its premium advertising programme across the board.

My take

This case changed how platforms sell creators to advertisers. After it, being in a premium tier meant being reviewed, not just being big.

Dublin, Ireland / Travel and hospitality

Elle Darby and the White Moose Café, 2018

The situation

Hotels and restaurants received a growing stream of requests from influencers offering coverage in return for free stays and meals, with little agreement on what that exposure was worth.

The move

Elle Darby emailed the White Moose Café and Charleville Lodge asking for five free nights in exchange for videos and posts. The owner, Paul Stenson, posted her email publicly with her name removed and a scathing reply.

  • Elle Darby A YouTube audience and a common style of collaboration request.
  • Paul Stenson A combative social media style and a following that loved a fight.

The turning point

Darby was identified and posted a tearful video. The owner banned all bloggers and sent her a mock invoice. The row went round the world and became shorthand for the question of what influence is worth.

My take

This row is the industry’s awkward adolescence in one email. The fix it pointed to, clear rate cards and audience data, is now standard practice.

Anaheim, United States / Events

Tana Mongeau, 2018

The situation

VidCon, in Edition 01, had become the creator industry’s main event. Some creators felt it treated them badly and wanted their own events built around their fans.

The move

Tana Mongeau announced TanaCon, a free convention with paid VIP tickets, held at a hotel near VidCon in Anaheim in June 2018 and organised with a small event company.

  • Tana Mongeau A big young audience and a promise of an event that put fans first.
  • The organisers A venue far too small for the crowd that turned up.

The turning point

Thousands of fans queued for hours in the heat, the venue could not cope, and the event was shut down on its first day. Mongeau apologised and promised refunds.

My take

TanaCon is VidCon’s cautionary twin. Both were creator-led. One had the patience to start small, the other started with the size of its audience.

London, United Kingdom / Advertising regulation

Competition and Markets Authority, 2019

The situation

British advertising rules required paid posts to be labelled, but many celebrities and influencers were vague, using tags that viewers didn’t notice or understand.

The move

The Competition and Markets Authority investigated and secured formal commitments from sixteen celebrities and influencers, including Ellie Goulding and Rita Ora, to disclose clearly when they had been paid or given products.

  • The sixteen Recognisable names whose commitments set an example.
  • The CMA Consumer protection law, and the power to take court action if commitments were broken.

The turning point

In January 2019 the commitments were published alongside new guidance. Clear labels like Ad became the expected standard in the UK, and the regulator later acted against platforms too.

My take

This is the British moment influencer marketing grew up. It turned disclosure from good manners into law that individuals could be held to.

Washington DC, United States / Online video and privacy

Federal Trade Commission, 2019

The situation

YouTube said it was not for under-13s, but channels for children drew huge audiences and personalised adverts ran on their videos, using data from young viewers.

The move

The Federal Trade Commission and New York’s attorney general alleged that YouTube had collected children’s data without parental consent. Google and YouTube agreed to pay $170 million.

  • Children’s creators Some of the most watched content on the platform.
  • The regulators Children’s privacy law, and a ruling that creators could be liable too.

The turning point

From January 2020 creators had to label videos made for children, which turned off personalised adverts, comments and some features. Many children’s creators reported steep falls in income.

My take

This case moved the rules for children’s content from the platform to every creator. It is a reminder that audiences come with obligations.

Los Angeles, United States / Comedy and online video

Jenna Mourey, 2020

The situation

In 2020 many creators faced scrutiny over offensive material in their older videos. Jenna Mourey, known as Jenna Marbles, had been on YouTube for a decade and was widely admired.

The move

In June 2020 she posted a video apologising for past content, including a sketch in which she impersonated Nicki Minaj and another with offensive jokes, and said she was leaving her channel.

  • Jenna Marbles Ten years of comedy and a reputation for kindness to her community.
  • Her audience Loyal viewers, many of whom asked her to stay.

The turning point

She made private many old videos and stopped posting. The channel stayed silent, and her decision became a reference point in conversations about accountability among creators.

My take

This is the rare failure case where the creator set the terms. It is also a reminder that ten years of public work carries ten years of different standards.

12 2022TikTok / Creator product Chef Pii / 2022 / OwnershipThe Pink SauceA private chef went viral with a bright pink sauce and started selling it. The labels and the shipping went wrong in public.TikTok · Relaunched Summer 2022From viral launch to retail partnership · legacy factArchival

Miami, United States / Food

Veronica Shaw, 2022

The situation

TikTok made it possible for anyone to build demand for a product in days. Making food safely at scale, with correct labels and reliable shipping, still took time and expertise.

The move

Veronica Shaw, known as Chef Pii, posted videos of a vivid pink condiment and sold bottles online. Buyers reported leaking packages, inconsistent colour and nutrition labels that did not add up.

  • Chef Pii A product the internet couldn’t stop talking about, and the courage to sell it herself.
  • Dave’s Gourmet An established sauce maker that later partnered with her to produce it properly.

The turning point

The criticism became its own TikTok trend in July 2022. Within weeks she partnered with Dave’s Gourmet, which reformulated and produced the sauce, and it went on sale in Walmart.

My take

Pink Sauce went from failure to retail in a summer. The recovery is the lesson: the creator brought the audience, and the partner brought the competence.

Massachusetts, United States / Beauty

Mikayla Nogueira, 2023

The situation

Mikayla Nogueira had built a large audience on honest, affordable beauty reviews. Sponsored reviews were common, and audiences relied on creators to show real results.

The move

In January 2023 she posted a sponsored video for L’Oréal’s Telescopic Lift mascara. Viewers and other creators argued the result looked like false lashes, which she did not confirm or address directly.

  • Mikayla Nogueira Millions of followers who trusted her reviews.
  • L’Oréal A paid partnership for a product the review had to show honestly.

The turning point

The video became a wider argument about trust in sponsored beauty content, nicknamed lashgate. The mascara still sold well, and the debate raised questions about advertising rules for before-and-after demonstrations.

My take

This case shows how fast an audience will test a creator’s honesty. The trust she had built was the reason it mattered so much.

Global / Shopping and affiliate marketing

MegaLag, 2024

The situation

Honey, a browser extension owned by PayPal, had been sponsored by many of the biggest YouTubers, from MrBeast to Linus Tech Tips. Creators also earned through affiliate links.

The move

In December 2024 the YouTuber MegaLag published an investigation alleging that Honey replaced creators’ affiliate tags with its own at checkout, taking commissions that creators believed were theirs.

  • MegaLag Months of research and a clear demonstration of how the extension worked.
  • The creators Years of sponsorships that had put Honey in front of millions.

The turning point

Millions of users uninstalled the extension within weeks, creators who had promoted it withdrew support, and lawsuits followed, including one led by the YouTuber LegalEagle.

My take

This is the creator economy investigating itself, and it worked. A single well-researched video did what years of sponsorship scrutiny hadn’t.

United States / Short video

TikTok, 2025

The situation

A US law required TikTok to be sold by its Chinese owner or banned from 19 January 2025. Creators had built businesses on it, and many had not built elsewhere.

The move

Late on 18 January 2025 TikTok went offline in the United States, showing users a message about the law. Creators posted goodbyes and urged followers to find them on other platforms.

  • TikTok’s American creators Audiences they could only reach through one app.
  • The US government A law that put the platform’s future in political hands.

The turning point

Service returned within about fourteen hours after President Trump said he would delay enforcement. The shutdown made creators diversify, and the platform’s future in the US stayed uncertain for months.

My take

India’s ban in Edition 04 showed what happens when a platform disappears. America got fourteen hours of the same lesson, and most of the industry took it seriously.

04 / The evidence

What a failure can and can’t prove

Moment, milestone, result

A first upload or a record is a moment. A milestone, like a subscriber count, shows attention. Only a result shows what changed for the creator, the platform or a brand. Each case says which of the three the record supports.

Whose number it is

Many figures come from the platforms, the creators themselves or the press. The source for every outcome is named, so you can judge it. Estimates of creator income are marked as estimates.

Attention is not income

Views, subscribers and concurrent viewers count attention. Paid subscriptions, sales and chart positions get closer to money. The rating says which kind of number a case rests on.

The evidence rating

Every case gets one of five ratings, strongest first: modelled effect, sales trend, survey, reach only and archival. A date or a deal term is marked as a legacy fact so it isn’t read as a result.

The creator comes first

Each case puts the creator at the centre: what they made, what changed because of it, and who else was involved. Brand partnerships appear when the creator drove them.

How cases earn a place

Each case changed what was possible for creators, platforms or brands, and left a public record. Creator stories already told in Brand Turning Points or Partnership Turning Points are not repeated here; The Index links to them.
