01 / The finding

Famous creator moments prove attention, not income.

None of these fifteen moments has a modelled effect. Six rest on attention alone: views, subscribers, a crowd at a convention or a peak audience on a stream. Five are archival, a date or an award that marks the moment without measuring it.

Four get close to money. A family sold a home video for $760,999. Two YouTubers attracted an estimated 773,000 paid streams of a boxing match. A meme gave Lil Nas X a record 19 weeks at number one, and Kai Cenat’s audience paid for more than a million subscriptions in a month.

The pattern is consistent. The creator economy is very good at counting its audience and much less good at showing what that audience did next. The later editions look for creators and partners who measured the second part.

A note on scope

What Edition 01 leans on

All 15 cases run from 2004 to 2025, and eleven start in North America, because the moments most people remember happened on American platforms first. Two are from Britain, one from South Korea and one from Sweden and Britain. The finale, Edition 07, looks deliberately beyond them. Not every creator moment leaves a number behind. Where it doesn’t, I say so, and I rate the record for what it is.

01 Jawed Karim / 2005 / FormatMe at the ZooA co-founder stood in front of the elephants and said almost nothing. It was the first video on YouTube.YouTube · First upload 23 April 2005The date the first YouTube video went live · legacy factArchival

San Diego, United States / Online video

Jawed Karim, 2005

The situation

In early 2005, putting video online meant wrestling with file formats, plug-ins and hosting bills. Most people who filmed something had nowhere easy to share it, and nobody thought of an ordinary person with a camera as a broadcaster.

The move

Jawed Karim, one of YouTube’s three founders, uploaded a short clip of himself at San Diego Zoo, filmed by a friend. He talks about the elephants’ trunks and the clip ends.

  • Jawed Karim A test upload, with no script, no edit and no audience in mind.
  • YouTube A page that played video in the browser, with no download and no player to install.

The turning point

The clip went up on 23 April 2005 and is still live on Karim’s channel. Within eighteen months Google bought YouTube for $1.65 billion in stock, and the idea that anyone could publish video became the default.

My take

I love that the most important video in the creator economy is deliberately unremarkable. It is a proof of plumbing. Everything in this archive runs through the pipe it tested.

What to take into a creator programme

The first version of a format rarely looks like the future. Judge new platforms by how easy they make it to publish, not by the quality of the early content.

How I’d measure it now

For a format test, measure friction rather than fame: time from camera to published page, share of uploads that play first time, and how many first-time uploaders post a second video within a month.

02 Gary Brolsma / 2004 / FormatThe Numa Numa DanceA teenager lip-synced to a Moldovan pop song at his desk. It was a TikTok, fifteen years early.Web · Viral before YouTube December 2004When the clip was posted to Newgrounds · legacy factArchival

New Jersey, United States / Online video and music

Gary Brolsma, 2004

The situation

Before YouTube, viral video lived on animation portals and forwarded emails. A clip spread only if someone hosted the file and someone else passed the link on.

The move

Gary Brolsma filmed himself on a webcam miming and dancing in his chair to O-Zone’s Dragostea din tei, and posted it to Newgrounds in December 2004.

  • Gary Brolsma A face, a webcam and complete commitment to the song.
  • Newgrounds A portal that hosted the file, and a community that forwarded it everywhere.

The turning point

By early 2005 the clip was everywhere, and The New York Times wrote about Brolsma as an unwilling celebrity. He stepped back from the attention, then returned in 2006 with a sequel and a contest inviting others to make their own versions.

My take

This is the template for half of short-form video: a person, a camera, a borrowed song and no shame. It also shows the cost of fame without consent, which the industry still hasn’t solved.

03 lonelygirl15 / 2006 / FormatThe Vlogger Who Was WrittenMillions followed a lonely teenager’s bedroom diaries. Then fans proved she was an actress.YouTube · Unmasked September 2006When the series was revealed as fiction · legacy factArchival

Los Angeles, United States / Online video and drama

Flinders, Beckett and Goodfried, 2006

The situation

In 2006 the confessional vlog was YouTube’s signature genre. Audiences assumed what they saw was real, and nobody had yet tried to tell a scripted story inside that assumption.

The move

Filmmakers Mesh Flinders and Miles Beckett, with lawyer Greg Goodfried, cast Jessica Lee Rose as Bree, a home-schooled teenager posting under the name lonelygirl15, and released the story one vlog at a time.

  • The creators A serialised mystery written to look unscripted.
  • The audience Detective work, theories and comment threads that pushed the series further.

The turning point

In September 2006 fans and journalists traced the videos back to the production team and identified Rose. Instead of ending, the series carried on as openly fictional, and its makers built a company around web drama with brands written into the story.

My take

lonelygirl15 invented the creator-led drama and the branded web series in one go. It also gave us the first big lesson in disclosure, years before regulators wrote the rules down.

04 OK Go / 2006 / FormatHere It Goes AgainFour musicians, eight treadmills, one take. A band became its own broadcaster.YouTube · Grammy Grammy, 2007Best Short Form Music Video · legacy factArchival

Los Angeles, United States / Music and online video

OK Go and Trish Sie, 2006

The situation

Music videos in 2006 were expensive, made by labels for television, and judged on whether MTV played them. YouTube was where fans uploaded copies, not where bands launched work.

The move

OK Go choreographed a routine on eight treadmills with Trish Sie, frontman Damian Kulash’s sister, filmed it in a single continuous shot and released it straight to YouTube.

  • OK Go An idea that cost little and could only be understood by watching to the end.
  • YouTube An audience that shared links and a player that let anyone embed the video.

The turning point

The video spread worldwide and won the Grammy for Best Short Form Music Video in February 2007. Later, when the band’s label restricted embedding to protect its income, OK Go left the label and kept making videos as an independent act.

My take

OK Go are the bridge between the music industry and the creator economy. They proved a band could build an audience from its own uploads, and their fight over embedding was an early sign that platforms and rights holders wanted different things.

05 The Davies-Carr family / 2007 / MonetisationCharlie Bit My FingerA father filmed his sons for their godfather. The clip paid for their education.YouTube · Sold as an NFT $760,999NFT sale price, May 2021Sales trend

England, United Kingdom / Online video and family content

Howard Davies-Carr, 2007

The situation

In 2007 home videos were made for relatives. The idea that a family clip could earn money for years, let alone be sold as an asset, did not exist.

The move

Howard Davies-Carr uploaded 56 seconds of his younger son Charlie biting his older brother Harry’s finger, so family abroad could see it. The clip became one of YouTube’s most watched videos, and the family later earned from advertising and licensing.

  • The Davies-Carr family An unplanned moment that anyone could relate to in seconds.
  • YouTube Advertising revenue sharing that turned old uploads into lasting income.

The turning point

In May 2021 the family auctioned the clip as an NFT, which sold for $760,999. They said at first the video would come down from YouTube, then decided to keep it up.

My take

This case runs from family archive to collectible in fourteen years. It is also the one that should make every brand and platform think hard about children, consent and who owns a moment.

06 Justin Bieber / 2008 / DiscoveryFound by AccidentHis mother posted his covers for family. A manager clicked the wrong video and found a star.YouTube · Record deal 2008The year the record deal was signed · legacy factArchival

Stratford, Ontario, Canada / Music and online video

Pattie Mallette and Justin Bieber, 2007 to 2008

The situation

Record labels found talent through scouts, showcases and demo tapes. A boy singing covers in small-town Ontario had no route to an industry gatekeeper.

The move

Pattie Mallette uploaded videos of her son singing, including at a local talent contest, so relatives could watch. Scooter Braun came across one in 2007, tracked the family down and brought Bieber to Atlanta.

  • Justin Bieber Talent, a following built one cover at a time, and evidence that people would watch.
  • Scooter Braun Industry access, a manager’s plan and introductions to Usher and the labels.

The turning point

In 2008 Bieber signed to a venture between Braun and Usher with Island Def Jam. His first single followed in 2009, and YouTube had produced its first global pop star.

My take

This is the case that rewired music marketing. After Bieber, every label had a person watching YouTube, and every young singer knew the bedroom could be the audition.

07 Lucas Cruikshank / 2009 / CrossoverThe First MillionA teenager with a sped-up voice became the first YouTuber with a million subscribers, then got a film.YouTube · Milestone 1 millionSubscribers, the first channel to get thereReach only

Nebraska, United States / Comedy and online video

Lucas Cruikshank, 2008 to 2009

The situation

By 2008 YouTube had stars, but subscribers were not yet a currency. Television networks still saw online video as a place for clips, not a source of talent.

The move

Lucas Cruikshank created Fred Figglehorn, a hyperactive six-year-old with a pitched-up voice, in short sketches aimed squarely at children and young teens.

  • Lucas Cruikshank A character, a release rhythm and a young audience that subscribed.
  • Nickelodeon A network that saw the subscriber count as proof of demand and made a film.

The turning point

In April 2009 Fred became the first channel to reach a million subscribers, and YouTube marked it on its official blog. Nickelodeon released Fred: The Movie in 2010 and a series followed.

My take

Fred is where subscribers became a currency the old industry understood. It is also an early reminder that an audience built for three-minute sketches does not always follow a creator to ninety minutes.

08 Hank and John Green / 2010 / CommunityVidConTwo brothers booked a hotel basement for online video fans. It became the creator economy’s annual meeting.YouTube · Launched 1,400People at the first VidCon, 2010Reach only

Los Angeles, United States / Online video and events

Hank and John Green, 2010

The situation

By 2010 YouTube had a community of creators and fans, but no place where they met, and the industry around them had no shared calendar or trade event.

The move

Hank and John Green, the brothers behind the Vlogbrothers channel, organised a convention where creators, fans and the businesses forming around them could meet in person.

  • Hank and John Green A loyal community, credibility with creators and the energy to organise it themselves.
  • Creators and fans Attendance, and the willingness to travel for something they had only seen on screens.

The turning point

About 1,400 people came to the first VidCon in a hotel ballroom in Century City. Within a decade it filled the Anaheim Convention Center, and in 2018 Viacom owned it.

My take

VidCon is when creators stopped being a curiosity and became an industry with a trade show. It was built by creators for their community first, which is why the industry turned up.

09 PSY / 2012 / DiscoveryGangnam StyleA Korean satire of status became the first video to pass a billion views, then broke the counter.YouTube · First billion 1 billionViews, the first video to get thereReach only

Seoul, South Korea / Music and online video

PSY, 2012

The situation

In 2012 a hit still needed radio and television in each market. K-pop had a passionate audience abroad, but the idea of a Korean-language song topping charts in the West seemed remote.

The move

PSY released a comic video mocking the flashy lifestyle of Seoul’s Gangnam district, with a horse-riding dance anyone could copy. Fans, parodies and reaction videos carried it around the world.

  • PSY A song, a dance that worked in any language and a willingness to be the joke.
  • The audience Parodies, flash mobs and reactions that turned one video into thousands.

The turning point

On 21 December 2012 it became the first video to pass one billion views on YouTube. In 2014 it neared the counter’s limit of 2,147,483,647 and YouTube upgraded the counter to cope.

My take

Gangnam Style showed that a global audience no longer needed translating. The audience did the distribution, and the platform found out its own systems had not imagined that scale.

10 PewDiePie / 2013 / CommunityThe Biggest Person on YouTubeA Swedish student filmed himself playing horror games. He became the most subscribed person on the platform.YouTube · Most subscribed 100 millionSubscribers, August 2019, the first individual creatorReach only

Sweden and United Kingdom / Gaming and online video

Felix Kjellberg, 2010 to 2019

The situation

Gaming videos in the early 2010s were mostly walkthroughs and reviews. Personality was a bonus, and few thought anyone would watch someone simply react to a game.

The move

Felix Kjellberg posted let’s play videos built around his own reactions and running jokes, and spoke to his viewers as a group with its own name, the Bro Army.

  • Felix Kjellberg Daily uploads, a recognisable voice and a direct relationship with fans.
  • His audience Loyalty that behaved like a movement, from in-jokes to subscription drives.

The turning point

In 2013 his channel became the most subscribed on YouTube. When the music label T-Series threatened that position in 2018 and 2019, fans campaigned to keep him on top, and in August 2019 he became the first individual creator to reach 100 million subscribers.

My take

PewDiePie made the personality the product. He is also the clearest example of the creator economy’s central tension: the same closeness that builds loyalty makes the creator answerable for things a brand would never sign off.

11 Ninja / 2018 / LiveDrake Joins the SquadA streamer played Fortnite at 1am with the biggest rapper in the world. Twitch had never seen a crowd like it.Twitch · Record 635,000Peak concurrent viewers, a Twitch record at the timeReach only

United States / Gaming and live streaming

Ninja and Drake, 2018

The situation

Live streaming was a gaming subculture in 2018. Mainstream celebrities did not stream, and the biggest Twitch audiences came from esports tournaments, not one person’s channel.

The move

Tyler Blevins, known as Ninja, played Fortnite live with Drake, joined later by Travis Scott and the American footballer JuJu Smith-Schuster. Nothing was planned beyond the game.

  • Ninja Skill, a big loyal audience and a laid-back show anyone could drop into.
  • Drake Global fame, and the curiosity of fans who had never watched a stream.

The turning point

The stream on 15 March 2018 peaked at about 635,000 concurrent viewers, a record for a single streamer on Twitch at the time. Within months celebrities, athletes and brands were queuing to appear on streams.

My take

This was the night live streaming became mainstream entertainment. It worked because Drake turned up as a player, on Ninja’s terms. The brands that followed and tried to stage it rarely matched it.

KSI and Logan Paul, 2018

The situation

Boxing events were sold by promoters and television networks. YouTube creators had huge audiences, but nobody had asked them to pay for a live event.

The move

Olajide Olatunji, known as KSI, and Logan Paul turned a rivalry into a white-collar boxing match, promoted it entirely through their own channels and sold access through YouTube’s pay-per-view.

  • KSI and Logan Paul A rivalry their audiences cared about and months of content to build it.
  • YouTube Paid live streaming at a scale it had rarely tested.

The turning point

The fight at Manchester Arena on 25 August 2018 drew around 15,000 people and 773,000 official paid streams at £7.50. It ended in a draw, a rematch followed, and creator boxing became an industry.

My take

This is the night creators found out they could sell tickets, not just ads. The piracy is the footnote that matters: a paywall is only as good as its weakest stream.

05 / The evidence

What a creator moment can and can’t prove

Moment, milestone, result

A first upload or a record is a moment. A milestone, like a subscriber count, shows attention. Only a result shows what changed for the creator, the platform or a brand. Each case says which of the three the record supports.

Whose number it is

Many figures come from the platforms, the creators themselves or the press. The source for every outcome is named, so you can judge it. Estimates of creator income are marked as estimates.

Attention is not income

Views, subscribers and concurrent viewers count attention. Paid subscriptions, sales and chart positions get closer to money. The rating says which kind of number a case rests on.

The evidence rating

Every case gets one of five ratings, strongest first: modelled effect, sales trend, survey, reach only and archival. A date or a deal term is marked as a legacy fact so it isn’t read as a result.

The creator comes first

Each case puts the creator at the centre: what they made, what changed because of it, and who else was involved. Brand partnerships appear when the creator drove them.

How cases earn a place

Each case changed what was possible for creators, platforms or brands, and left a public record. Creator stories already told in Brand Turning Points or Partnership Turning Points are not repeated here; The Index links to them.
