About this archive

Creator Turning Points is the third archive, alongside Brand Turning Points and Partnership Turning Points. It puts the creator at the centre: what they made, what became possible and which part of the outcome can be traced.

Seven editions contain 100 moments from 2002 to 2025. The evidence ranges from original uploads and public rule changes to chart positions, donations and reported sales. Every case separates its media record from its outcome source, and gives the headline number a definition.

Creators rarely work alone. Performers, editors, developers, moderators, production teams and commercial partners help make the work possible. The two contributions in each case name part of that collaboration, rather than an exhaustive production credit.

This is a selection, not a ranking or a representative sample. The rating describes what the public record supports. It does not rate a creator’s talent or a programme’s worth. With no modelled effects or representative surveys in this selection, the lessons are questions to test in a future programme.

03 / The lessons

Six lessons that hold across editions

None of these belongs to one edition. Each is backed by cases from at least two angles. Choose a case to read it in full.

01

Creators find the use before the platform does.

Platforms build features. Creators work out what they are for. A programmer turned Twitch chat into a controller, a mother in a car park showed Facebook what live video could be, and a factory worker’s shrug became TikTok’s most followed account.

04 Twitch Plays Pokémon04 Candace Payne04 DJ Khaled01 Gary Brolsma07 Khaby Lame

02

Attention is easy to count. Proof is rare.

None of the 100 cases has a modelled effect. 24 rest on attention alone and 48 are archival. The best proof came from outside the creator economy: book tills, grocery baskets and company results.

02 Kate Allinson and Kay Featherstone02 Emily Mariko02 Colleen Hoover and BookTok01 PSY06 Hot Ones

03

Revenue that grows with the audience lasts.

Fixed funds paid each creator less as more joined, and were replaced. Shared advertising and money paid directly by fans grew with the audience, and became the creator economy’s foundations.

03 YouTube’s first partners03 TikTok’s creators03 Jack Conte04 Snapchat Spotlight creators01 Kai Cenat

04

Own a line to your audience that no platform controls.

Vine closed, India banned TikTok, America lost it for fourteen hours and advertisers left YouTube overnight. The creators who recovered fastest could reach their audience somewhere else.

05 Vine’s creators04 India’s TikTok creators05 America’s TikTok creators05 YouTube’s creators03 Alex Cooper

05

The creators who lasted own something.

Ownership is joint top of the archive’s nine shifts, with Format and Trust, at 16 cases each. A subscription, a newsletter, a chocolate bar or a company bought back from its owners turned an audience into an asset.

06 MrBeast06 Michelle Phan06 Smosh03 Bill Bishop07 Austin Li

06

Trust is the asset, and it can be spent.

Trust is the shift behind 16 cases. A reviewer’s verdict sank a gadget and lifted a cleanser. The same credibility, lent to a fake festival or a doubtful sponsor, cost creators their audiences’ faith.

06 / What’s missing

What the series doesn’t cover yet

Where the work began

14 cases use UK or Irish working locations and 60 North American locations. Europe has 4, Asia 11, Latin America 1, Africa 2 and Oceania 2. Locations describe the moment, not nationality or the full audience geography.

When the record begins

91 of 100 cases date from 2010 or later. Earlier web communities, non-digital creator practices and lost uploads are underrepresented. This is a selected digital archive rather than a complete history.

What the evidence can support

27 cases show a Sales trend, 24 Reach only and 48 Archival evidence. There are 0 Modelled effect and 1 Survey cases. Those absences limit any causal conclusion about creator effectiveness.

Whose work is less visible

Public records favour successful, English-searchable examples. Unpaid labour, smaller creators, private contracts, platform moderation and production teams are harder to see. Gross pledges and platform counts reveal less about sustainable net income than the headlines suggest.
