Why this edition exists

Edition 02 showed that creators move what people buy. Edition 03 asks a simpler question: how did creators themselves get paid? It follows the money through advertising shares, fan funding, acquisitions, platform funds and nine-figure contracts, and notes who kept the value each time.

01 / The finding

The money moved from platforms to fans, then back again.

Six of these fifteen cases are fans paying creators directly: a $1.19 million album, $8.8 million for a card game, $11.4 million for a cartoon, monthly memberships on Patreon, paid newsletters and paid messages in live chat. Four are platforms sharing their revenue, and four are companies buying creators or their audiences.

Ten of the fifteen are archival: a launch, a deal or a published estimate. Five rest on money actually paid, and the strongest are the fan-funded totals, because every dollar was a decision by a fan.

The pattern runs both ways. Fixed funds paid less as more creators joined, and acquired networks lost the creators they bought. Revenue that grows with the audience, from fans or from shared advertising, is what lasted.

01 YouTube’s first partners / 2007 / MonetisationThe Day Creators Got PaidYouTube offered its most popular uploaders a share of the advertising. Making videos became a job.YouTube · Launched May 2007When YouTube first shared advertising revenue with creators · legacy factArchival

San Bruno, United States / Online video

YouTube, 2007

The situation

By 2007 YouTube had enormous audiences and almost no way for the people making videos to earn from them. Popular uploaders were being courted by television and could easily have left.

The move

In May 2007 YouTube invited a small group of its most popular creators, including Smosh, LisaNova and lonelygirl15, into a Partner Program that placed adverts around their videos and shared the revenue with them.

  • The first partners Audiences big enough to sell to advertisers, and a reason to keep uploading.
  • YouTube Advertising sales and a payment system that reached individuals, not only companies.

The turning point

The programme widened through 2007 and later opened to almost anyone who met its thresholds. Sharing advertising with ordinary uploaders became the founding business model of the creator economy.

My take

Every creator business in this archive rests on this decision. YouTube chose to treat uploaders as partners rather than free labour, and its competitors spent the next fifteen years catching up.

02 Amanda Palmer / 2012 / CommunityTheatre Is EvilA musician left her label and asked her fans for $100,000. They gave her twelve times that.Kickstarter · Record raised $1.19 millionRaised from almost 25,000 backersSales trend

Boston, United States / Music

Amanda Palmer, 2012

The situation

Musicians relied on record labels to fund albums and tours. Amanda Palmer had left hers after disagreements over how her work was sold, and had a dedicated fanbase she spoke to directly online.

The move

Palmer asked fans on Kickstarter to fund an album, book and tour, offering tiers from a digital download to house concerts and art.

  • Amanda Palmer Years of direct contact with fans, and rewards that offered access as well as music.
  • Her fans Pledges from almost 25,000 people who wanted to fund the work themselves.

The turning point

The campaign closed in June 2012 with $1,192,793, against a goal of $100,000, the most for a music project on Kickstarter at the time. It became the template for fan-funded music.

My take

Palmer showed that a direct relationship with fans is a balance sheet. She also faced criticism for asking volunteer musicians to play for free, a reminder that fan funding raises expectations of fairness.

03 Jack Conte / 2013 / MonetisationA Musician Builds PatreonHis videos had millions of views and paid him a few hundred dollars. So he built a way for fans to pay monthly.Patreon · Founded $10 billionPaid to creators by fans on Patreon, as of 2025Sales trend

San Francisco, United States / Creator payments

Jack Conte and Sam Yam, 2013

The situation

Advertising revenue rewarded huge view counts. Creators with smaller, devoted audiences, or work that took months to make, could not live on it.

The move

Jack Conte, one half of the band Pomplamoose, spent months on an elaborate music video and earned very little from advertising. With his college roommate Sam Yam, he founded Patreon, where fans pledge a regular amount to support a creator.

  • Jack Conte First-hand knowledge of the gap between views and income, and credibility with other creators.
  • Patrons Small monthly payments that added up to a living for creators they cared about.

The turning point

Patreon launched in 2013. By 2025 it had passed $10 billion paid to creators by their fans, with more than $2 billion a year flowing through it.

My take

Patreon is the creator economy’s most important idea after advertising sharing: that fans want to pay. It was built by a creator who had felt the problem, which is why creators trusted it.

04 Maker Studios / 2014 / OwnershipDisney Buys a YouTube NetworkA network of YouTubers founded by creators sold to Disney for at least $500 million. Within three years most of them were gone.YouTube · Acquired $500 millionUpfront price, with up to $950 million in total · legacy factArchival

Los Angeles, United States / Online video and media

Maker Studios and Disney, 2014

The situation

Multi-channel networks bundled thousands of YouTube channels, sold advertising across them and took a share of creators’ income. Media companies wanted young audiences and saw networks as a shortcut.

The move

Maker Studios, founded by YouTubers including Lisa Donovan, Ben Donovan, Danny Zappin and Shay Carl, grew into one of the largest networks. Disney agreed to buy it for $500 million, with up to $450 million more if targets were met.

  • Maker Studios Tens of thousands of channels and a pipeline of young talent.
  • Disney Capital, characters and a plan to bring its brands to YouTube audiences.

The turning point

The deal in March 2014 made creator networks look like the future of media. By 2017 Disney had cut Maker from tens of thousands of channels to around 300 and folded it into a new division.

My take

Maker is the moment old media tried to buy the creator economy whole. What it learned is that a network is only as strong as the creators’ willingness to stay in it.

05 Twitch’s streamers / 2014 / LiveAmazon Buys TwitchPeople watching other people play games looked like a niche. Amazon paid almost a billion dollars in cash for it.Twitch · Acquired $970 millionPrice Amazon paid in cash, August 2014 · legacy factArchival

San Francisco, United States / Live streaming

Twitch and Amazon, 2014

The situation

Twitch grew out of Justin.tv, a site built around one founder streaming his life. Its gaming section took off as players broadcast their games and audiences gathered to chat and subscribe.

The move

Streamers built communities that watched for hours at a time, paying for subscriptions and emotes. Twitch, run by Emmett Shear and Justin Kan’s team, became the home of live gaming.

  • Twitch’s streamers Hours of live entertainment every day and fiercely loyal chat communities.
  • Amazon Cash, infrastructure and a long-term bet on live video.

The turning point

In August 2014 Amazon bought Twitch for $970 million in cash, after reports that Google had been close to a deal. Live streaming was confirmed as a format worth owning.

My take

Amazon paid for a habit that streamers had created. Ten years on, the fights over revenue splits and exclusive deals all trace back to that imbalance.

06 The Oatmeal / 2015 / CommunityExploding KittensA webcomic artist and two game designers asked for $10,000 for a card game. More than 219,000 people backed it.Kickstarter · Most backed 219,382Backers, the most of any Kickstarter project at the timeSales trend

Seattle, United States / Games

Matthew Inman, Elan Lee and Shane Small, 2015

The situation

Board and card games reached shops through publishers. Crowdfunding was growing, but most campaigns aimed for tens of thousands of dollars.

The move

Matthew Inman, creator of the webcomic The Oatmeal, made a card game with designers Elan Lee and Shane Small and launched it on Kickstarter with his drawings and humour.

  • Matthew Inman A huge readership and an art style instantly recognisable to them.
  • Elan Lee and Shane Small Game design that made a simple idea playable.

The turning point

The campaign ended in February 2015 with $8,782,571 from 219,382 backers, the most backers of any Kickstarter project at that point. Exploding Kittens became a games company with retail distribution and a Netflix series.

My take

The Oatmeal turned readers into customers for a game, not a comic. It shows that what an audience trusts is the creator’s taste, which travels further than the original medium.

Global / Online video

Forbes, 2015

The situation

By 2015 YouTube stars were famous, but their income was a mystery. Parents, advertisers and the press assumed they earned little beyond pocket money.

The move

Forbes estimated the pre-tax earnings of YouTube’s biggest stars from advertising, sponsorships, merchandise and other deals, and published its first ranking.

  • The creators Businesses big enough to estimate, from advertising to books and tours.
  • Forbes A familiar rich-list format that made creator income headline news.

The turning point

PewDiePie came first with an estimated $12 million, ahead of Smosh, Fine Bros and Lindsey Stirling. The annual list made creator earnings a story the mainstream press followed every year.

My take

The rich list did for creators what box-office charts did for films: it gave everyone a number to argue about. It also helped persuade a generation that making videos was a career.

08 YouTube’s live streamers / 2017 / LiveSuper ChatViewers could pay to make their comment stand out. Live chat became a till.YouTube · Launched January 2017When Super Chat launched · legacy factArchival

Global / Live streaming

YouTube, 2017

The situation

Twitch streamers earned from tips and subscriptions while they broadcast. YouTube’s live streamers had only advertising and a little-used fan-funding button.

The move

In January 2017 YouTube launched Super Chat, letting viewers pay to highlight and pin their message in a live stream’s chat, with the money shared with the creator. It replaced the old fan-funding feature.

  • Live streamers Shows where reading and reacting to chat was part of the entertainment.
  • YouTube Payments built into the chat, available in many countries at once.

The turning point

Super Chat made paying a creator a public, social act. It became a core income for live streamers on YouTube and, later, for virtual YouTubers, whose fans gave some of the largest totals on the platform.

My take

Super Chat turned tipping into a performance. The creators who did best treated every paid message as a moment in the show, not a transaction.

Washington DC, United States / Newsletters

Bill Bishop and Substack, 2017

The situation

Writers depended on publications for income. Independent newsletters existed, but charging for one meant stitching together payment tools and email software.

The move

Bill Bishop, who had written Sinocism, a newsletter on China, for years, became the first writer to launch a paid publication on Substack, a new service built to make subscription newsletters simple.

  • Bill Bishop Deep expertise and readers who relied on his daily analysis.
  • Substack Payments and publishing in one place, taking a share of subscription revenue.

The turning point

Substack launched publicly with Sinocism in October 2017. Within a few years it had thousands of paid newsletters, and writers left newsrooms to build their own subscription businesses.

My take

Sinocism proves the creator economy is not only video. A writer with expertise and a mailing list owns his audience in a way no YouTuber ever fully can.

Los Angeles, United States / Animation and live streaming

Critical Role, 2019

The situation

Animated series were funded by studios and networks. A web show about a tabletop game had a devoted following but no obvious route to television.

The move

The cast of Critical Role, who streamed their campaign weekly, asked fans on Kickstarter to fund a 22-minute animated special based on their first story.

My take

Critical Role used crowdfunding as market research and won a series deal with the result. It is the cleanest example in the archive of a community proving demand before a company would.

11 TikTok’s creators / 2020 / MonetisationThe $200 Million Creator FundTikTok promised its creators $200 million. Many found the payments worth a few cents a thousand views.TikTok · Launched $200 millionInitial US fund, July 2020 · legacy factArchival

United States / Short video

TikTok, 2020

The situation

TikTok’s creators drove its growth but had little way to earn on the platform itself. Rivals were courting its stars, and the app faced political pressure in the United States.

The move

In July 2020 TikTok launched a $200 million fund for US creators, saying it would grow to $1 billion over three years, paid according to views and engagement.

  • TikTok’s creators The videos that kept hundreds of millions of people scrolling.
  • TikTok A fixed pot of money divided among eligible creators.

The turning point

Because the pot was fixed while the number of creators grew, many reported falling pay per view. TikTok replaced the fund in 2023 with a programme paying more for longer videos.

My take

The Creator Fund was a headline, not a business model. It is a useful warning for any brand or platform designing creator incentives: people work out quickly what they are worth.

Alex Cooper, 2021 to 2024

The situation

Call Her Daddy began in 2018 under Barstool Sports, which owned the show. When the host wanted better terms, the question of who owned a podcast’s name and audience became public.

The move

Alex Cooper won control of the show in 2021 and licensed it exclusively to Spotify in a deal reported at $60 million. In 2024 she moved to SiriusXM for a reported $125 million, keeping the rights to her show.

The turning point

The deals made Cooper one of the highest-paid podcasters and showed that the host, not the original publisher, held the value of a creator-led show.

My take

Cooper’s story is about ownership more than money. She fought to keep the name, and every later deal was possible because she did.

13 YouTube Shorts creators / 2023 / MonetisationShorts Get a ShareYouTube decided short videos would earn a share of advertising, not a bonus from a fund.YouTube · Launched 45%Share of the Shorts creator pool paid to creators · legacy factArchival

Global / Short video

YouTube, 2023

The situation

YouTube launched Shorts to compete with TikTok and paid creators through a $100 million fund. Like TikTok’s fund, it was a fixed pot and creators wanted something that grew with the platform.

The move

From 1 February 2023 YouTube pooled advertising revenue from Shorts and gave 45% of the creators’ portion to them, through the Partner Program, with new thresholds for eligibility.

  • Shorts creators Billions of daily views on short vertical videos.
  • YouTube An advertising system that already paid long-form creators, extended to a new format.

The turning point

Shorts moved from a fund to revenue sharing, setting a model that rivals had to answer. It tied creators’ income to the platform’s advertising growth.

My take

This is the Partner Program decision made again, sixteen years later, for a new format. It shows YouTube’s view that sharing advertising is what keeps creators.

Canada / Live streaming

xQc and Kick, 2023

The situation

Twitch dominated live streaming and its biggest streamers had few alternatives. New rivals needed audiences fast, and the quickest way was to pay for the streamers who brought them.

The move

Félix Lengyel, known as xQc and one of Twitch’s most-watched streamers, signed a two-year, non-exclusive deal with Kick reported at $70 million, rising to $100 million with incentives.

  • xQc Very long daily streams and one of the largest live audiences in the world.
  • Kick Guaranteed payment, a creator-friendly revenue split and freedom to stream elsewhere.

The turning point

The deal in June 2023 was recognised by Guinness World Records as the largest streaming deal for an individual. It forced other platforms to rethink how they paid and kept their streamers.

My take

This deal is the creator economy’s transfer fee. It also shows the questions to ask about where the money comes from before you follow it.

Global / Online video

YouTube, 2024

The situation

Creator income had grown into an economy, but platforms rarely disclosed what they paid. Creators, regulators and investors had to rely on estimates.

The move

In his February 2024 letter, YouTube’s chief executive Neal Mohan said the platform had paid more than $70 billion to creators, artists and media companies over the previous three years.

  • YouTube’s creators The videos, music and shows that generated the advertising and subscription revenue.
  • YouTube Advertising, YouTube Premium and the payment system built on the 2007 Partner Program.

The turning point

The figure put a scale on the business model that started in 2007. It also fed debate over how the money was shared between the largest media companies and individual creators.

My take

This closes the loop on the edition’s first case. The Partner Program became the largest creator payment system in the world, and the next argument is about how fairly it shares.

04 / The evidence

What a creator payment can and can’t prove

Moment, milestone, result

A first upload or a record is a moment. A milestone, like a subscriber count, shows attention. Only a result shows what changed for the creator, the platform or a brand. Each case says which of the three the record supports.

Whose number it is

Many figures come from the platforms, the creators themselves or the press. The source for every outcome is named, so you can judge it. Estimates of creator income are marked as estimates.

Attention is not income

Views, subscribers and concurrent viewers count attention. Paid subscriptions, sales and chart positions get closer to money. The rating says which kind of number a case rests on.

The evidence rating

Every case gets one of five ratings, strongest first: modelled effect, sales trend, survey, reach only and archival. A date or a deal term is marked as a legacy fact so it isn’t read as a result.

The creator comes first

Each case puts the creator at the centre: what they made, what changed because of it, and who else was involved. Brand partnerships appear when the creator drove them.

How cases earn a place

Each case changed what was possible for creators, platforms or brands, and left a public record. Creator stories already told in Brand Turning Points or Partnership Turning Points are not repeated here; The Index links to them.
